One Night

Leader guide: Entrusted in one night

God owns it all, we manage it, and He will ask for an account. Here is the whole course in 90 minutes.

Session pageHandout

Before you teach

  • Read Matthew 25:14-30 and Luke 16:1-13 twice.
  • Print a step card and handout for each person, and the scripture handout for anyone who wants to read ahead.
  • Decide who will read each passage aloud.
  • Practice the seven money-basics slides so each one takes about three minutes.
  • Mark your own step first so you can go first in discussion.

Caution

Do not try to teach all six steps in depth; the conversation is the point. The money basics are headlines, about three minutes each: explain the idea, show the picture, and move on. Give no specific investment recommendations. This version leaves out budgeting mechanics, loan detail and wills. Say so at the end and point people to the site or a longer track.

Teaching notes

Opening question

What is the best and the worst money decision you have made?

Teaching points

  1. Owner and manager.

    God owns all of it, and a manager is measured on faithfulness, not on the size of what he was handed. The two-bag servant hears the same words as the five-bag servant. The third servant's failure began with a wrong view of the master.

  2. Work and giving.

    Work is done for the Lord, and the ability to earn is a gift. The tithe is Old Testament law and a good practice; the New Testament instruction is to give what you have decided in your heart. If giving has never stretched you, pray over it more.

  3. The Baby Steps.

    This course follows Dave Ramsey's Baby Steps. Read the step card once. Most people are stuck on the first two: $1,000 for a starter emergency fund, and paying off all debt except the house with the debt snowball. Spend the time there, and have each person privately mark the step they are on.

  4. The long view.

    The master praises the shrewd manager's foresight, not his dishonesty, and Jesus applies it to using money for what lasts. $200 a month from age 22 reaches about $655,000 by 65 at an assumed 7% return, and about $309,000 if started at 32. Proverbs 13:22 looks two generations ahead; it is wisdom, not a guarantee.

    The 7% return is an assumption for illustration. Returns are not guaranteed.

Money basics

Show each slide's picture, say the one idea, and move on. The Money Basics page has more on each for questions.

  1. Compound interest: growth on growth.

    Your money earns a return, and then the return earns a return. Sam puts in $200 a month from 22 to 32, then stops: $24,000 in. Jordan puts in $200 a month from 32 to 65: $79,200 in. At an assumed 7%, Sam has about $346,000 at 65 and Jordan about $309,000. Ten early years beat thirty-three later ones.

    The 7% return is an assumption for illustration. Returns are not guaranteed.

  2. The rule of 72.

    Divide 72 by the yearly return to see about how many years it takes money to double. At 7%, that is roughly every 10 years. $10,000 at age 22 becomes about $20,000 at 32, $40,000 at 42, $81,000 at 52 and $163,000 at 62, without adding a dollar. The last doubling is worth more than all the ones before it.

    The 7% return is an assumption for illustration. Returns are not guaranteed.

  3. It works against you too.

    On a $5,000 card balance at 24% APR, the first month's interest is $100. Pay $100 a month and the balance never goes down. Pay $200 a month and it is gone in 36 months, after $2,001 in interest. Compound interest is a great servant and a hard master, which is why Baby Step 2 comes before investing.

  4. APR is what you pay. APY is what you earn.

    APR is the yearly rate on a loan or card, before compounding. APY is the yearly rate on savings, including compounding. A card at 24% APR that compounds daily costs about 27.1% a year. Savings at 4% compounded daily earn a 4.08% APY. When you borrow, compare APRs. When you save, compare APYs.

  5. How income tax brackets work.

    Federal income tax is charged in layers called brackets, and each rate applies only to the slice of income inside its layer. Take a single filer with $60,000 of taxable income and round-number brackets: 10% on the first $12,000, 12% on the next $36,000 and 22% on the last $12,000. That is $8,160 in all, or 13.6% of the $60,000. The 22% is the marginal rate, what the next dollar costs. A raise never makes you take home less.

  6. Your 401(k) and the match.

    A 401(k) is a retirement account through your job. Money comes out of each paycheck before you see it and goes into funds you choose from the plan's list. Many employers add a match. For example, on a $50,000 salary you put in 6%, or $3,000 a year, and an employer that matches half of the first 6% adds $1,500. That is an instant 50% return. Ask HR what your match is.

  7. What the stock market is.

    A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. The stock market is where people buy and sell those pieces, and prices move every day with how the companies are doing and how people feel about them. An index fund owns a small piece of hundreds of companies at once, the kind of spreading out Ecclesiastes 11:2 describes. Any single year can drop sharply, so money you need in the next few years belongs in savings.

Practical step: Find your step

Mark the Baby Step you are on. Write down the one thing that is stopping the next one. Take the inventory home and fill it in this week.

Discussion questions

  1. Which Baby Step are you on, and what is stopping the next one?
  2. Where do you most naturally think "this is mine"?
  3. For couples: do you agree with each other on which step you are on?
  4. What is one thing you will do in the next 30 days?

Take-home action

  • Fill in the "What I manage" inventory at home this week.
  • Do the one thing you wrote down within 30 days, and tell someone you trust when it is done.
  • Find out whether your employer matches retirement contributions, and make sure you get all of it.

Likely objections

That was a lot. Where do I start?
With the inventory and one step. Nothing else this week.
We disagree as a couple.
Normal. Fill in the inventory together before discussing any decision.
Which fund should I pick in my 401(k)?
This course does not recommend specific investments. Learn what an index fund and a target-date fund are, compare the fees in your plan, and talk to a qualified advisor about your situation.
