Money basics

The practical side of the course: how compound interest works for you and against you, APR and APY, how income tax brackets work, the 401(k) and its match, and what the stock market is. These are the headlines from the One Night session, with a little more on each.

Compound interest: growth on growth.

Your money earns a return, and then the return earns a return. Sam puts in $200 a month from 22 to 32, then stops: $24,000 in. Jordan puts in $200 a month from 32 to 65: $79,200 in. At an assumed 7%, Sam has about $346,000 at 65 and Jordan about $309,000. Ten early years beat thirty-three later ones.

The 7% return is an assumption for illustration. Returns are not guaranteed.

Nothing about Sam is special except the start date. For the first decade the balance looks like it is barely moving; the curve bends upward late, and it only bends for money that is already in.

The same idea runs in reverse when you borrow, which is why the debt snowball comes before investing in the Baby Steps.

The rule of 72.

Divide 72 by the yearly return to see about how many years it takes money to double. At 7%, that is roughly every 10 years. $10,000 at age 22 becomes about $20,000 at 32, $40,000 at 42, $81,000 at 52 and $163,000 at 62, without adding a dollar. The last doubling is worth more than all the ones before it.

The 7% return is an assumption for illustration. Returns are not guaranteed.

It works for debt too. A card at 24% doubles what you owe in about three years if you pay nothing.

The rule is a shortcut, not exact math, and real returns move around from year to year.

It works against you too.

On a $5,000 card balance at 24% APR, the first month's interest is $100. Pay $100 a month and the balance never goes down. Pay $200 a month and it is gone in 36 months, after $2,001 in interest. Compound interest is a great servant and a hard master, which is why Baby Step 2 comes before investing.

Minimum payments on a card are set so the balance lasts for years. Anything you add above the minimum goes straight to the balance.

APR is what you pay. APY is what you earn.

APR is the yearly rate on a loan or card, before compounding. APY is the yearly rate on savings, including compounding. A card at 24% APR that compounds daily costs about 27.1% a year. Savings at 4% compounded daily earn a 4.08% APY. When you borrow, compare APRs. When you save, compare APYs.

Lenders show you the smaller number and banks advertise the bigger one; both are telling the truth, they are just measuring differently.

On a car loan or mortgage, the APR also includes some fees, so it can be a little higher than the interest rate. Compare APRs between lenders, not monthly payments.

How income tax brackets work.

Federal income tax is charged in layers called brackets, and each rate applies only to the slice of income inside its layer. Take a single filer with $60,000 of taxable income and round-number brackets: 10% on the first $12,000, 12% on the next $36,000 and 22% on the last $12,000. That is $8,160 in all, or 13.6% of the $60,000. The 22% is the marginal rate, what the next dollar costs. A raise never makes you take home less.

The numbers above are rounded for teaching. The real brackets, and the standard deduction, change most years, so look up the current ones before you plan.

Taxable income is not your paycheck. Tax is figured on income after deductions. Everyone gets a standard deduction, and money you put into a traditional 401(k) comes off before the bracket math.

Marginal and effective. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar. Your effective rate is total tax divided by total income, and it is always lower. Use the marginal rate to judge a raise, a side job or a traditional-versus-Roth choice, and the effective rate to see what you really pay.

Deduction or credit. A deduction lowers the income that is taxed. A credit lowers the tax itself, dollar for dollar, so a $1,000 credit is worth more than a $1,000 deduction.

Payroll tax is separate. Social Security (6.2%, up to a yearly wage limit) and Medicare (1.45%) come out of each paycheck whatever your bracket, and your employer pays a matching share.

Withholding and refunds. Your employer withholds tax from each paycheck using your Form W-4. A large refund means you lent the government money interest-free, and owing a lot means too little was withheld. Look at your W-4 after a raise, a marriage or a second job.

Traditional or Roth. A traditional account saves tax now, at your marginal rate, and you pay it when you withdraw. A Roth is the other way around. A low rate now, as in a first job, often favors Roth; a high rate now can favor traditional.

Scripture asks for honest taxes. Paul says to give taxes to whom taxes are owed (Romans 13:6-7), and Jesus tells his questioners to give to Caesar what is Caesar's (Matthew 22:21). Using the deductions and credits the law offers is good management. Hiding income is not.

For your own return, use the IRS tools and a tax professional you trust. This course explains how the system works and does not give tax advice.

Your 401(k) and the match.

A 401(k) is a retirement account through your job. Money comes out of each paycheck before you see it and goes into funds you choose from the plan's list. Many employers add a match. For example, on a $50,000 salary you put in 6%, or $3,000 a year, and an employer that matches half of the first 6% adds $1,500. That is an instant 50% return. Ask HR what your match is.

Traditional or Roth. With a traditional 401(k) you skip the income tax now and pay it when you take the money out. With a Roth 401(k) you pay the tax now and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Many plans offer both.

Vesting. Some matches become fully yours only after you have worked there a certain number of years. Your own contributions are always yours. Ask HR for the vesting schedule.

IRA. An individual retirement account is one you open yourself, separate from any job. It also comes in traditional and Roth versions.

Limits. The IRS sets yearly contribution limits and changes them most years. For most people they are well above 15% of pay, but look up the current numbers before you plan.

What the stock market is.

A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. The stock market is where people buy and sell those pieces, and prices move every day with how the companies are doing and how people feel about them. An index fund owns a small piece of hundreds of companies at once, the kind of spreading out Ecclesiastes 11:2 describes. Any single year can drop sharply, so money you need in the next few years belongs in savings.

Over long stretches, owning productive businesses has grown wealth, with deep drops along the way. Past returns do not guarantee future ones, which is why this course uses a conservative 7% for its examples.

Bonds are loans to a company or government that pay interest. They usually move less than stocks and usually earn less.

Target-date funds are a common 401(k) choice: one fund holding a mix of stocks and bonds that gets more conservative as the year in its name gets closer.

Every fund charges a yearly fee called the expense ratio. A difference of one percentage point a year sounds small and compounds into a large amount over decades, so it is worth comparing.

Never do something with your money that you do not understand. If you cannot explain in a sentence what you own and how it makes money, wait. The prudent think about where their steps lead (Proverbs 14:15).

If you want help, find someone with the heart of a teacher. A good adviser explains what you are buying, what it costs and what could go wrong, and welcomes the same question twice. Be wary of anyone who rushes you, gets vague about fees, or sells you something you cannot explain back.

Cheat sheets

Loans and rates cheat sheet

TermWhat it means
PrincipalThe amount you borrowed and still owe.
InterestWhat the lender charges for the use of the money.
APRAnnual percentage rate: the yearly rate on a loan, before compounding. This is the number you pay.
APYAnnual percentage yield: the yearly rate including compounding. This is the number you earn on savings.
Why both existA card at 24% APR that compounds daily really costs about 27% a year. Lenders show the smaller number, banks advertise the bigger one.
TermHow long the loan lasts. A longer term means a lower payment and more total interest.
Minimum paymentThe least you can pay without a penalty. On a card it is set so the balance lasts for years.
Co-signingYou owe the whole debt if the other person stops paying. See Proverbs 22:26-27.

Retirement accounts cheat sheet

AccountHow it works
401(k) or 403(b)Offered through an employer. Money comes out of your paycheck automatically. Often comes with a match.
TraditionalYou skip the tax now and pay it when you take the money out in retirement.
RothYou pay the tax now, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Often a good fit when your income is low, as in a first job.
IRAA retirement account you open yourself, separate from any employer. Comes in traditional and Roth versions.
Index fundOne fund that owns a small piece of hundreds of companies. Built-in diversification at low cost.
Contribution limitsSet by the IRS and changed most years. Look up the current numbers before you plan.
